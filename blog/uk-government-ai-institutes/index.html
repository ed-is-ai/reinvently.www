UK Government AI Institutes: Roles, Funding and Gaps

The UK's public AI institutions fund research, evaluate leading models, support adoption and translate technology into practice. This guide maps their roles, funding and remaining gaps.

← All posts

Key Takeaways

  • The Alan Turing Institute, founded in 2015 at the British Library by five universities, now has over a hundred university partners funded partly through EPSRC.
  • The AI Security Institute, created in 2023 after the Bletchley Park summit, was the world's first national body evaluating frontier AI models, working directly with OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind.
  • UKRI channels £8.8 billion in 2025-26, rising toward £10 billion by 2030, into UK research, with EPSRC funding most of the country's academic AI work.
  • NHS England cut NHS AI Lab funding from £250 million to £143.5 million and wound the programme down, though its clinical AI frameworks still set the UK standard.
  • Despite leading on AI safety governance, the UK still trails US cloud providers on compute capacity and struggles to commercialise publicly funded research at home.

Discussion of the UK's AI ambitions usually focuses on startups, universities or funding announcements. Less attention goes to the public institutions between academia, government and industry. They translate research into practice, set standards, evaluate risks and support organisations that lack their own resources.

These institutions are not household names. Many technologists recognise the Alan Turing Institute, but fewer know its role. The AI Security Institute made headlines in 2023, yet its daily work is less visible. The same is true of the Hartree Centre, Digital Catapult and NHS AI Lab.

This guide maps what each institution does, why it exists and where it fits. Their scope, budget and ambition vary widely. Together, however, they represent substantial public investment in responsible AI capability. The aim is to extend access beyond organisations able to build everything themselves.

How do the UK's public AI institutions fit together?

  • Research and standards: the Alan Turing Institute connects academic research with public policy and applied programmes.
  • Safety evaluation: the AI Security Institute tests the capabilities and risks of leading models.
  • Research funding: UKRI and EPSRC distribute public investment across universities, institutes and businesses.
  • Sector adoption: the NHS AI Lab's legacy frameworks cover health, while the Hartree Centre supports industrial computing and AI.
  • Commercial translation: Digital Catapult and Innovate UK help businesses test and adopt emerging technology.
  • Responsible adoption: the former CDEI, now the Responsible Technology Adoption Unit, develops practical governance guidance.

The Alan Turing Institute

London  ·  National Institute for Data Science and AI

The Alan Turing Institute is the UK's national institute for data science and AI. Founded in 2015, it is based at the British Library in London. It takes its name from the mathematician, codebreaker and computing pioneer Alan Turing.

Cambridge, Edinburgh, Oxford, UCL and Warwick founded the Institute with EPSRC funding. It now has more than a hundred university partners. Its work spans research, skills and training, and public policy.

In practice, the Institute connects academic researchers, government departments and industry partners. Its work ranges from AI ethics and fairness to defence and health data science. Training programmes also develop data-science capability across the public sector.

One of its most visible public contributions has been on AI safety and standards. It publishes research and guidance on topics like algorithmic transparency, bias in machine learning systems, and the responsible deployment of AI in high-stakes settings like criminal justice and healthcare. This work has shaped policy thinking in the UK and internationally.

AI Security Institute (AISI)

London  ·  AI Safety and Evaluation

The AI Security Institute launched as the AI Safety Institute in 2023. It was the world's first dedicated national AI safety body. The Institute sits within DSIT and followed the Bletchley Park AI Safety Summit.

Its mission is to evaluate leading models before wide deployment. Tests cover dangerous capabilities, including assistance with biological or chemical weapons. They also assess deception and resistance to human oversight.

The Institute conducts pre-deployment evaluations with developers including OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind. It publishes findings and methods that contribute to international assessment practice. The US, EU and Japan have since established equivalent bodies.

The AISI's significance is disproportionate to its size. With a relatively small team by government standards, it has established the UK as a genuine leader in AI safety governance — a position that carries real international influence as debates about how to regulate the most powerful AI systems intensify.

UKRI and EPSRC

Swindon  ·  Research Funding

UK Research and Innovation oversees public research investment across the UK. Its combined budget was £8.8 billion in 2025–26 and is due to approach £10 billion by 2030. Within UKRI, the Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council funds much of the country's academic AI research.

EPSRC grants supported foundational work behind the UK's commercial AI ecosystem. This includes deep learning at Edinburgh, natural-language processing at Cambridge and computer vision at Oxford. Researchers who later led companies such as DeepMind, Darktrace and PolyAI often began on EPSRC-funded projects.

Innovate UK plays a different role within UKRI. It provides grants and loans to businesses developing or adopting new technology. For early-stage AI companies, this can fund research and development before revenue is sufficient.

UKRI is not a visible institution in the way that the Turing Institute or the AI Security Institute are — most people outside research will never encounter it directly. But as the primary mechanism through which public money reaches the people doing foundational AI research, it is arguably the most important public institution in the UK AI ecosystem.

NHS AI Lab

London  ·  AI in Health and Care

The NHS AI Lab was established within NHS England to accelerate the safe and responsible adoption of AI across health and care. It funds and evaluates AI tools for clinical use, develops standards and frameworks for AI in healthcare, and runs programmes to build AI capability among NHS clinicians and managers.

Healthcare is both an important and difficult AI application. Potential benefits include faster diagnosis, better treatment selection and earlier detection of deterioration. The risks are also high. NHS scale, complexity and data infrastructure create challenges beyond ordinary software deployment.

The Lab funded the AI and Digital Regulations Service with NICE, the CQC, MHRA and HRA. The service guides developers and NHS organisations through clinical evaluation and approval. Research projects also covered cancer detection, sepsis prediction and diabetic-eye screening.

NHS England later scaled the programme back. A 2022 spending review cut funding from £250 million to £143.5 million, and the Lab has wound down as a distinct programme. Its frameworks remain important reference points for UK clinical AI.

For anyone building AI tools for the healthcare sector, the Lab's legacy still matters: its frameworks set the standard for what responsible AI deployment in health looks like in the UK, and they are increasingly referenced internationally.

The Hartree Centre

Daresbury, Cheshire  ·  Industrial AI and High-Performance Computing

The Hartree Centre sits within the Science and Technology Facilities Council (STFC), part of UKRI, and is based at Daresbury Laboratory in Cheshire. It focuses on the application of high-performance computing, data analytics and AI to industrial problems — particularly in manufacturing, materials science and energy.

The Hartree Centre helps UK businesses access computing power and AI expertise that might otherwise be out of reach. Its work ranges from materials simulation in advanced manufacturing to predictive maintenance for energy infrastructure.

The Hartree Centre is less visible than some of the other institutes on this list, partly because its work is technical and sector-specific rather than policy-facing. But for the parts of the UK economy that most need AI to improve productivity — manufacturing, logistics, materials — it is an important resource.

Digital Catapult

London (with regional centres)  ·  AI and Digital Innovation for Business

The Digital Catapult is a government-backed innovation centre, part of the UK's network of Catapult centres, which are designed to bridge the gap between research and commercial application in specific technology areas. The Digital Catapult's focus is on advanced digital technologies, including AI, immersive technology and future networks.

Digital Catapult primarily serves small and medium-sized enterprises. These firms may lack the expertise or risk tolerance to experiment alone. Facilities, specialists and industry networks let them explore AI without building every capability in-house.

Collaborative programmes bring companies, universities and government bodies together around shared problems. They develop proofs of concept, test technology in real environments and feed results into policy. For many SMEs, this is the most accessible route into the wider innovation system.

Centre for Data Ethics and Innovation (CDEI)

London  ·  Responsible AI and Data Governance

The Centre for Data Ethics and Innovation was established in 2018. In 2024, it became the Responsible Technology Adoption Unit within DSIT. It advises government, studies emerging ethical issues and publishes practical guidance for organisations deploying AI.

Some of the CDEI's most influential work has been on algorithmic transparency — helping organisations understand and communicate how their automated decision-making systems work, particularly in contexts like recruitment, benefits administration and policing where the stakes for individuals are high. It has also done significant work on data portability, online targeting and the governance of AI in the public sector.

The CDEI occupies an interesting position: it is part of government but operates with a degree of independence, and its reports often acknowledge tensions and trade-offs that purely political bodies might prefer to avoid. For anyone trying to understand where UK AI policy is heading — or trying to build AI systems that will hold up to scrutiny — its publications are worth reading.

Which institution should an organisation engage?

  • For academic research or standards work: start with the Alan Turing Institute or an EPSRC-funded university partnership.
  • For research and development funding: examine UKRI, EPSRC and Innovate UK programmes.
  • For industrial computing and technical expertise: the Hartree Centre is the most directly relevant route.
  • For an SME testing emerging technology: Digital Catapult offers facilities, specialists and collaborative programmes.
  • For clinical AI: use the NHS AI Lab's legacy evaluation frameworks and the AI and Digital Regulations Service.
  • For governance and responsible adoption: consult guidance from the Responsible Technology Adoption Unit and relevant sector regulator.

What does the public AI infrastructure get right—and where are the gaps?

Taken together, these institutes represent a serious attempt to build public AI infrastructure. Some of it is working well.

The UK has genuine strengths in AI safety and governance. The AI Security Institute was the first of its kind. The CDEI has produced influential responsible-AI policy work, while the Turing Institute connects academia with public applications. The NHS AI Lab addressed deployment in a large, complex and risk-sensitive institution.

Important gaps remain. The AI Research Resource programme adds domestic compute, but US cloud capacity remains much larger. Commercial translation is also slow. EPSRC-funded university work can reach US companies faster than British ones.

The institutional landscape is fragmented, with separate mandates and funding structures. France and Singapore present more coherent national strategies.

These gaps do not diminish what has been built. The institutions set standards, fund research, evaluate risk and provide practical support. Anyone working in UK AI should understand how to use them, even when they are absent from the headlines.

Frequently asked questions

What are the UK's main government AI institutes?

The Alan Turing Institute (national institute for data science and AI), the AI Security Institute (safety evaluation of leading models), UKRI and EPSRC (research funding), the NHS AI Lab, the Hartree Centre (industrial AI and high-performance computing), the Digital Catapult (AI for SMEs) and the Centre for Data Ethics and Innovation.

What does the AI Security Institute do?

Established in 2023, originally as the AI Safety Institute within DSIT after the Bletchley Park summit, it was the first national AI safety body in the world. It evaluates the capabilities and risks of leading models before deployment, working directly with developers including OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind.

What is the Alan Turing Institute?

The UK's national institute for data science and AI, founded in 2015 at the British Library by five universities with EPSRC funding. It connects academia, government and industry and has shaped UK and international thinking on AI ethics, fairness and safety.

Where does the UK's public AI infrastructure fall short?

Domestic compute capacity remains well behind US cloud providers, the commercial translation of publicly funded research is slow, and the landscape of institutes can feel fragmented compared with more coherent national strategies elsewhere.
