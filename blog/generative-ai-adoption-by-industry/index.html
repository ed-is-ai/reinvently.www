Generative AI Adoption by Industry: Where It Is Being Used, What Is Working, and What Comes Next

88% of organisations now use AI in at least one business function. But aggregate statistics obscure the reality on the ground. Here is a sector-by-sector breakdown of what is actually happening.

Key Takeaways

  • Adoption rates depend on what is measured: any AI use, regular generative AI use and employee use of agents are different metrics.
  • McKinsey's July 2024 survey, published in 2025, found 71% of respondents reporting regular organisational generative AI use. It is not a 2026 survey.
  • Cambridge's 2026 report found more than 80% of surveyed financial firms adopting AI at some level. That includes AI beyond generative models.
  • KPMG's banking figure of 56% concerns employee adoption of AI agents, not the proportion of all banks deploying them.
  • Use the source table below to compare definitions and dates before using a percentage in a business case.

Generative AI is used for drafting, summarisation, coding and knowledge retrieval across industries. The evidence below separates adoption surveys from examples of possible applications and reported financial returns. A high usage rate does not establish a productivity gain.

But aggregate statistics obscure the reality on the ground. Adoption is uneven. Some industries are running production AI systems that have measurably transformed their operations. Others are running pilots that have yet to find a business case. And within industries, the gap between the most and least advanced organisations is widening rapidly.

This post is a sector-by-sector breakdown of where generative AI is actually being used and what use cases are delivering the most value. It also looks at what the adoption data tells us about where things are heading.

Financial Services — Adoption and Integration

Cambridge's 2026 report describes widespread AI use alongside an execution gap: experimentation does not establish institution-wide integration. Its financial-sector findings should not be used to rank finance against sectors surveyed by other researchers.

Applications to evaluate

Fraud detection and credit scoring often use predictive models rather than generative AI. Keep those systems separate when assessing generative AI adoption.

Fraud detection and risk Predictive models can flag unusual transactions for investigation. Evaluate false positives, missed fraud and investigator effort; do not count all fraud-detection systems as generative AI.

Credit and risk scoring Unstructured information can supplement a credit workflow, but any proposed model needs validation for the decision and population it affects.

Regulatory and compliance Potential generative AI applications include drafting summaries of regulatory changes and assisting document review. Check each summary against its source and retain an accountable reviewer.

Customer service and advisory Assisted replies and knowledge retrieval can be evaluated against resolution time, incorrect answers and escalation quality. Customer-facing advice needs controls appropriate to its consequences.

AI agents. KPMG's Q2 2026 US survey reports employee adoption rising from 23% to 56% in banking. This is an executive-reported workforce metric from large institutions, not a count of banks with production agent systems.

What is still hard

Explainability in credit decisions remains a regulatory challenge. High-stakes automated advice is still heavily governed. The compliance overhead of deploying AI in regulated functions is real, and smaller institutions often lack the resources to navigate it.

Healthcare — Documentation and Clinical Validation

Market-size forecasts do not tell us how many healthcare organisations use generative AI. The applications below range from documentation support to clinical systems with different validation needs; they should not be combined into one adoption claim.

Applications to evaluate

Clinical documentation has emerged as an important near-term use case. Ambient documentation tools can listen to patient consultations and draft structured notes. Pilot programmes report physicians recovering hours of clinical time each week.

Medical imaging and diagnostics has seen accelerating adoption, with AI tools now reading radiology scans, detecting anomalies and flagging cases for urgent review in a growing number of NHS trusts and hospital networks.

Patient communication and triage is evolving rapidly. A growing share of major healthcare networks now use AI-powered chatbots to handle initial patient enquiries, freeing clinical staff for higher-complexity work.

Drug discovery and research is perhaps the most exciting frontier. Generative models are being used to propose novel molecular structures, predict protein interactions and dramatically accelerate the early stages of drug development pipelines.

What is still hard

Patient data governance is genuinely complex. Integration with legacy clinical systems remains a significant barrier. The consequences of errors in clinical AI are severe, which means validation and regulatory approval processes are — rightly — demanding.

Retail and E-commerce — Speed and Personalisation at Scale

The generative AI retail market was valued at over $1 billion in 2025 and is growing rapidly. Growth is driven by the industry's intense competitive pressure on personalisation, efficiency and speed-to-market.

Applications to evaluate

Product content and copywriting was one of the earliest and most straightforward use cases. Generating product descriptions, category pages and marketing copy at scale — across thousands of SKUs, in multiple languages, for multiple channels — is a task that generative AI does well.

Personalised recommendations and messaging has moved beyond collaborative filtering into generative territory, with systems that compose personalised outreach and adjust promotional messaging to individual customer segments in real time.

Visual merchandising and creative is emerging as a significant use case. AI image generation is being used to create product imagery variations, lifestyle photography and seasonal campaign assets at a fraction of the cost of traditional photography.

Customer service at retail scale — handling returns, tracking enquiries, product queries — is heavily automated, with generative AI handling the long tail of customer interactions.

What is still hard

Maintaining brand voice and quality at AI generation scale requires systematic evaluation and human review at every stage. Hallucinated product specifications are a real and embarrassing risk.

Legal and Professional Services — Research and Review

Thomson Reuters reported legal generative AI usage rising from 14% in 2024 to 26% in its April 2025 publication. These are survey results, not evidence that every legal workflow is ready for autonomous use.

Applications to evaluate

Document review and due diligence can use generative AI to locate clauses and draft summaries. Evaluate retrieval coverage and factual accuracy against a reviewed sample before relying on the output.

Legal research requires checking generated citations and interpretations against the original authorities. A fluent answer is not evidence that the cited case exists or supports the proposition.

Contract drafting and review is evolving rapidly. Agentic systems can now review an uploaded agreement, identify clauses that deviate from standard templates, flag risk areas, and produce a redlined version — without step-by-step prompting.

What is still hard

Hallucination in legal research is a serious risk — there have been high-profile cases of AI-generated citations that do not exist. Privilege and confidentiality concerns around client data entering third-party AI systems remain live issues.

Manufacturing and Engineering — Efficiency at the Operational Level

Applications to evaluate

Predictive maintenance and visual defect detection below are examples of wider AI use; they do not establish generative AI adoption.

Predictive maintenance uses AI to identify patterns in machine sensor data that precede failures, allowing maintenance to be scheduled before breakdowns occur. The ROI in reduced downtime is often compelling enough to justify substantial investment.

Quality control and defect detection — computer vision models monitoring production lines in real time — has become standard in advanced manufacturing environments.

Design and engineering assistance is an emerging use case where generative AI helps engineers explore design variations, generate documentation and identify potential failure modes earlier in the development cycle.

What is still hard

Legacy operational technology systems in factories are often decades old and not designed for AI integration. The gap between what is technically possible and what is safely deployable in regulated manufacturing environments remains significant.

Education — Early but Accelerating

Personalised tutoring is the most promising long-term use case. AI systems that adapt to an individual student's pace, learning style and knowledge gaps have shown strong results in pilot programmes. The UK government is investing in AI tutoring tools specifically aimed at supporting disadvantaged pupils.

Teacher productivity — lesson planning, resource creation, marking assistance, differentiation of materials — is already delivering time savings for teachers willing to adopt AI tools.

Assessment and feedback at scale is being explored by universities and examining bodies, though with significant caution around assessment integrity.

Non-profit and Charity — High Promise, Significant Barriers

The 2025 Charity Digital Skills Report, based on 672 participating charities, found 76% using AI tools. This is UK survey evidence about AI broadly, not a worldwide generative AI deployment rate.

The same report highlights skills, funding and governance constraints. Tool use alone does not show that AI is embedded in an organisation's service delivery or strategy.

Applications to evaluate

Fundraising and donor engagement attracts the greatest interest and some of the most tangible effects. Reported uses include predictive analytics, personalised outreach and automated engagement. AI can help gift officers prioritise portfolios, draft communications and identify lapsed donors likely to re-engage.

Grant writing and reporting consumes substantial charity capacity. Generative AI can draft applications, tailor proposals and produce impact reports. For smaller organisations without dedicated fundraising staff, the resulting time saving can materially expand what the team can attempt.

Content and communications — social media, email campaigns, website copy, volunteer recruitment materials — is an area where even the smallest charity can immediately benefit. Generating a first draft in seconds rather than hours is a meaningful productivity gain for teams where everyone is stretched.

Service delivery innovation is where the most ambitious use cases are emerging. Spring ACT's Sophia chatbot, for example, assists survivors of domestic violence in more than 140 countries, providing 24/7 anonymous support in dozens of languages — a scale of reach that no human team could replicate.

What is still hard

Before adopting a tool, identify who can evaluate its output and maintain it within the charity's budget. Treat donor, volunteer and beneficiary information according to the organisation's data policies.

Test the tone and accuracy of donor communications with the people responsible for those relationships. Drafting assistance and sending unreviewed messages create different risks.

Google.org's Generative AI Accelerator — a six-month programme for selected non-profits providing up to $2 million in equivalent support, Google Cloud credits and technical training — is one example of the funded routes now available. For UK charities, the AI Skills Hub and Charity Digital both publish accessible resources for organisations beginning their AI journey.

The sector's values need not block AI adoption. They can guide it instead.

Which adoption patterns repeat across industries?

Looking across industries, several consistent patterns emerge:

Drafting is only one stage. Faster first drafts can shift work into checking and integration. Our AI coding productivity review illustrates why activity gains and delivered value need separate measurement.

Start with measurable work. Repeated tasks with explicit quality criteria make it easier to compare assisted and unassisted outcomes. High volume alone does not make a task safe or economical to automate.

Agentic applications are the next frontier — and now measurable. The shift from AI-as-assistant to AI-as-agent is in production: KPMG's Q2 2026 AI Pulse puts agent deployment at 53 percent of large US enterprises, with multi-agent orchestration doubling to 18 percent in a quarter. The governance behind it lags badly, a gap we examine in The Agent Governance Gap.

The gap between leaders and laggards is widening. Differences in data, skills and delivery capacity may affect outcomes. Cross-sectional performance comparisons do not establish that adopting earlier caused better results.

What Should Your Organisation Do?

Identify your highest-volume, most consistent tasks. These are your fastest wins. Where do your teams spend significant time on work that is repetitive, document-heavy or information-intensive? Start there.

Run a measurable pilot. Record current turnaround time, error rates and cost, then compare assisted work on similar tasks. Include review and rework. Agree quality thresholds and data-handling rules before considering production use.

Invest in evaluation. The difference between useful AI and embarrassing AI is usually the quality of your evaluation and human review processes. Build these before you scale.

Think about your data. The most powerful AI applications connect models to your organisation's own data. The organisations that have done the work of making their data accessible will extract dramatically more value from AI than those that have not.

Frequently asked questions

How widely is generative AI used in 2026?

The surveys cited here measure different populations and dates. McKinsey reported 71% regular organisational generative AI use from July 2024 fieldwork, published in 2025. It should not be described as a 2026 adoption estimate.

Which industries have adopted generative AI most?

The cited surveys do not establish a comparable ranking of industries. General AI use, generative AI use, employee use of agents and market growth are different measures. Compare definitions, populations and fieldwork dates before drawing conclusions.

Which use cases deliver the most value?

Document drafting, summarisation, coding and knowledge retrieval are candidates for evaluation. Their value depends on accuracy, review effort, integration and cost in the actual workflow; an adoption survey does not establish which use case delivers the greatest return.

What should organisations do first?

Choose a bounded workflow, record its current cost and quality, and run an assisted pilot. Include review and rework, agree data-handling rules and expand only when the results meet the quality and cost thresholds.
